%% ABSTRACT
%% ============================================================
\begin{abstract}
Influence maximization (IM) has been extensively studied under the
Independent Cascade (IC) and Linear Threshold (LT) models, where an
activated node broadcasts influence to all neighbors simultaneously.
Many real-world promotional campaigns, however, involve
\emph{non-replicable} coupons that can be redeemed once and passed to at
most one recipient at each step.
We formalize this setting as the \emph{Sequential Coupon Diffusion
Influence Maximization (SIDIM)} problem, in which $k$ coupons are
seeded at selected nodes and each propagates independently as a random
walk: at each visited node, the coupon is either adopted (activating the
user and terminating the walk), discarded, or forwarded to exactly one
neighbor.
The objective is to select $k$ seed nodes maximizing the expected number
of distinct users who adopt at least one coupon.
We prove SIDIM is NP-hard and establish that the adoption spread
function is monotone and submodular, admitting a greedy
$(1{-}1/e)$-approximation.
The core technical challenge is that evaluating marginal gains exactly
is \#P-hard, and classical RR-set construction does not extend to this
setting.
We address this by developing \emph{$k$-joint RR sets} — $k$
independent reverse random walks rooted at a common node — and prove
they yield an unbiased estimator of adoption spread.
Our \SIDIMRIS{} algorithm combines $k$-joint sampling with an
adaptive stopping criterion, achieving a
$(1/2{-}\epsilon)$-approximation in expected near-linear time.
Experiments on five real-world social networks show that \SIDIMRIS{}
consistently matches a Monte Carlo oracle and substantially outperforms
both structural heuristics and IC-model baselines, confirming both the
importance of the diffusion model formulation and the effectiveness of
the proposed algorithm.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Influence maximization (IM) is the problem of finding a subset of nodes
(seed nodes) in a social network that maximizes the expected spread of
influence~\cite{kempe2003maximizing}.
It is a well-studied problem with applications in viral marketing,
information diffusion, and social-network based
promotion~\cite{domingos2001mining,borgs2014maximizing,tang2015influence}.
While most studies treat influence maximization as a broadcast diffusion
problem, in this paper, we explore how to use influence maximization for
non-replicable coupons that can be redeemed once and forwarded to only
one recipient.

Consider the following motivating example.
A company launches a new food-delivery service in a city already served
by a large incumbent platform.
To acquire early users, the company distributes $k$ unique discount
coupons to selected customers.
A customer receiving a coupon may redeem it for a discounted order, let
it expire, or forward it to exactly one friend who may be more likely to
use it.
Once the coupon is redeemed, it is consumed; once it is forwarded, the
sender no longer holds another copy.
Thus the promotion does not create a broadcast cascade in which one
activated user simultaneously influences all neighbors.
Similar situations occur in closed-beta invitation campaigns, referral
codes tied to individual accounts, and donation vouchers that can be
claimed only once.
Different from classical viral marketing, the coupon is non-replicable,
meaning that each coupon exists as a single moving object in the social
network.
This constraint makes the coupon follow a forwarding chain rather than a
branching influence tree.
Therefore, coupon-based promotion needs to incorporate the
non-replicable nature of the coupon when deciding which users should
receive the initial coupons.

To formalize the above setting, we integrate non-replicable coupon
propagation with influence maximization into a coherent model called the
\emph{Sequential Coupon Diffusion (SCD)} model.
In particular, a directed social network $G=(V,E)$ specifies who may
forward a coupon to whom, while node-level probabilities specify whether
a user adopts, discards, or transfers a received coupon.
In the SCD model, a coupon seeded at node $u$ performs a sequential
random walk: at each visited node, it is either redeemed and stops,
discarded and stops, or forwarded to exactly one out-neighbor.
Thus the diffusion topology of one coupon is a path rather than a tree,
and the activation event is redemption rather than mere receipt.
More specifically, in a $k$-coupon campaign, we model the propagation of
$k$ coupons through $k$ independent possible worlds.
We use $S=(s_1,\ldots,s_k)$ to denote the seed tuple, where coupon $j$
starts from seed node $s_j$ and evolves according to its own possible
world.
A node contributes to the campaign objective if at least one coupon is
eventually redeemed by this node.
We define $\sigma(S)$ as the expected number of distinct adopting users
under seed tuple $S$.
The campaign task is defined as the following Sequential Coupon
Diffusion Influence Maximization (SIDIM) problem: given the network,
the adoption, discard, and forwarding probabilities, and a coupon budget
$k$, find $k$ seed nodes such that $\sigma(S)$ is maximized.

For the SIDIM problem, we show that selecting the optimal seed tuple is
NP-hard, while the adoption spread function is monotone and submodular,
indicating that a greedy algorithm gives a $(1{-}1/e)$ approximation if
the objective can be evaluated exactly.
However, exact evaluation of the adoption spread is \#P-hard, making
direct greedy optimization computationally infeasible on large social
networks.
To provide an efficient solution, we design \emph{$k$-joint reverse
reachable (RR) sets}, which sample $k$ independent reverse random walks
from a common root and give an unbiased estimator of the distinct
adoption probability.
We then develop a \SIDIMRIS{} algorithm based on the reverse influence
sampling approach and an adaptive stopping framework, and prove that it
achieves a $(1/2{-}\epsilon)$ approximation in expected near-linear time.
Our empirical evaluation further demonstrates that \SIDIMRIS{}
consistently matches a Monte Carlo oracle and outperforms coupon-aware
heuristics and IC-model baselines.

One important point we demonstrate both theoretically and empirically is
that coupon-based influence maximization is tightly dependent on the
non-replicable nature of the coupon.
This point indicates that SIDIM is quite different from classical
influence maximization: the campaign has to consider single-path
forwarding, adoption-versus-receipt semantics, and overlap among
independent coupons, while classical influence maximization mainly
targets increasing the spread of a branching activation cascade.

In summary, our contributions include: (a) proposing the SCD model that
captures non-replicable sequential coupon propagation; (b) defining the
SIDIM problem and studying its hardness, monotonicity, and submodularity;
and (c) designing the \SIDIMRIS{} algorithm based on $k$-joint RR sets to
solve SIDIM efficiently.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that treats
coupons as non-replicable objects in an influence
maximization framework and provides an algorithmic study of seed
selection under such a sequential diffusion model.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Influence maximization.}
The IM problem was formalized by Domingos and
Richardson~\cite{domingos2001mining,richardson2002mining} as
probabilistic inference over social networks.
Kempe, Kleinberg, and Tardos~\cite{kempe2003maximizing} gave the
foundational treatment: they proved IM is NP-hard under IC and LT,
showed that adoption spread is monotone and submodular, and established
a greedy $(1{-}1/e)$-approximation.
CELF~\cite{leskovec2007celf} and CELF++~\cite{goyal2011celf} reduced
the cost of greedy via lazy evaluation.
All of these methods assume \emph{broadcast} diffusion, in which an
activated node may simultaneously attempt to influence all neighbors;
they are structurally inapplicable to non-replicable coupon propagation.

\paragraph{Reverse influence sampling.}
Borgs et al.~\cite{borgs2014maximizing} introduced the RIS framework,
providing $(1{-}1/e{-}\epsilon)$-approximation in expected near-linear
time.
TIM/TIM+~\cite{tang2014influence} and IMM~\cite{tang2015influence}
developed practical algorithms with tight sample complexity.
SSA~\cite{nguyen2016stop} achieved asymptotically optimal bounds.
All of these methods construct a \emph{single} RR set per root,
corresponding to all broadcast paths to a target node in one possible
world.
In our coupon model, $k$ coupons propagate through $k$
\emph{independently sampled} worlds; a correct RR estimator must
generate $k$ separate reverse walks per root and pair each walk with its
own seed.
Classical single-RR-set construction either ignores this independence
(overcounting activations) or reduces to a single-coupon problem
(undercounting the union coverage).
Our $k$-joint RR sets resolve this gap.

\paragraph{Competitive and multi-item diffusion.}
A line of work studies competitive IM with multiple
items~\cite{budak2011limiting,chen2011influence,lu2015competition}, and
IM for simultaneous multi-item
seeding~\cite{bharathi2007competitive,goyal2012competitive}.
These models still employ broadcast diffusion for each item; competition
arises from nodes adopting at most one item among concurrently spreading
broadcasts.
In our model, items do not compete for a node — they simply propagate
via non-replicable walks, and the objective is to maximize the union of
reached users.
Sun et al.~\cite{sun2018multi} extend RIS to multi-round IM, which
similarly requires reasoning about $k$ independent propagation
instances; however, within each round, diffusion remains broadcast-style
IC.
None of these works handle non-replicable propagation.

\paragraph{Random-walk models in networks.}
Random walks underpin node centrality measures such as
PageRank~\cite{page1999pagerank} and have been used as heuristics for
seed selection.
However, these methods lack formal guarantees within an IM framework:
PageRank reflects stationary reachability, not adoption probability
under a given seed set.
Our work is the first to rigorously embed random-walk-style propagation
into the IM possible-world framework, establishing the Coverage
Lemma (Lemma~\ref{lem:coverage}) that connects reverse walks to
adoption events and enabling provably correct approximation guarantees.

\section{Model and Problem Definition}
\label{sec:model}

In this section, we introduce the Sequential Coupon Diffusion (SCD)
model, which integrates non-replicable coupons with
single-path random-walk propagation in social networks.
The SCD model describes how $k$ coupons, each issued to one seed user,
move through the network: a coupon can be adopted, discarded, or
transferred to exactly one neighbor, while the campaign counts distinct
users who eventually adopt at least one coupon.
Based on the SCD model, we define the Sequential Coupon Diffusion
Influence Maximization (SIDIM) problem, which characterizes how to
select the initial coupon recipients so that the expected adoption
spread is maximized.

\subsection{Sequential Coupon Diffusion (SCD) Model}

Sequential Coupon Diffusion (SCD) is a model for non-replicable
coupons that move through a social network one edge at a time, forming
a forwarding path rather than a broadcast cascade.
We model the social network as a directed graph $G=(V,E)$, where $V$ is
the set of $n=|V|$ users and $E$ is the set of $m=|E|$ directed
forwarding relationships.
Each node $u\in V$ has three behavior probabilities: an adoption
probability $p_u^a$, a discard probability $p_u^d$, and a transfer
probability $p_u^t$.
These probabilities are non-negative and satisfy
$p_u^a+p_u^d+p_u^t=1$.
Each edge $(u,v)\in E$ is associated with a forwarding probability
$p(u,v)$, indicating the probability mass with which user $u$ transfers
a coupon to user $v$.
A campaign starts by assigning $k$ coupons to $k$ seed users, and these
coupons then propagate independently according to the SCD process.

\paragraph{Single-coupon propagation.}
The propagation process of one coupon starts from a seed node
$s\in V$ in discrete time steps.
At step $\tau=0$, only node $s$ holds the coupon.
At step $\tau\geq 0$, suppose node $u$ holds the coupon.
Then $u$ adopts the coupon with probability $p_u^a$, in which
case the coupon is redeemed, node $u$ is counted as an activated user,
and the propagation terminates.
With probability $p_u^d$, node $u$ discards the coupon, in
which case the propagation also terminates but no new user is activated.
With the remaining probability $p_u^t=1-p_u^a-p_u^d$, node $u$
transfers the coupon to exactly one out-neighbor $v$, chosen according
to edge probabilities satisfying
$\sum_{v:(u,v)\in E}p(u,v)=p_u^t$.
The coupon then moves to $v$ and the same process repeats at the next
time step.
Under the possible-world view introduced below, the behavior of a node
is fixed for each coupon world; therefore, if a coupon enters a transfer
cycle without reaching an adopting node, it activates no additional user
and contributes zero to the objective.

One important distinction of the SCD model is that coupon propagation
and user activation are separated.
The propagation part is governed by the transfer action, where the
coupon can move to at most one neighbor in each step; the activation
part is governed by the adoption action, where only the user who redeems
the coupon is counted by the campaign.
Thus a forwarding user may help the coupon reach later users without
being activated, while an adopting user activates once and terminates
the coupon walk.
This distinction makes SCD fundamentally different from classical IC or
LT diffusion, where activation and propagation are coupled through a
branching cascade.

\paragraph{Possible-world formalization.}
To analyze the SCD model, we use the standard possible-world view of
random diffusion processes.
For every coupon $j\in[k]$, we independently instantiate one possible
world $W_j$ that fixes all random choices made by this coupon.
Specifically, for each node $u\in V$ and coupon $j$, we draw an
independent random number $r_{u,j}\sim\mathrm{Uniform}[0,1]$.
This random number determines the behavior of node $u$ when it receives
coupon $j$ as follows:
\[
  \mathrm{outcome}_{u,j} =
  \begin{cases}
    \text{adopt}          & \text{if } r_{u,j} \leq p_u^a, \\
    \text{discard}        & \text{if } r_{u,j} \in (p_u^a,\; p_u^a+p_u^d], \\
    \text{transfer to }v  & \text{if } r_{u,j} \text{ falls in } v\text{'s
                            sub-interval of } (p_u^a{+}p_u^d, 1],
  \end{cases}
\]
where the out-neighbors of $u$ are assigned disjoint sub-intervals
whose lengths are given by the edge probabilities $p(u,v)$.
The collection $W_j=\{r_{u,j}\}_{u\in V}$ is the possible world of
coupon $j$, and the tuple $\mathcal{W}=(W_1,\ldots,W_k)$ fully
determines the propagation outcome of all $k$ coupons.

\begin{definition}[Live edge]
Edge $(w,u) \in E$ is a \emph{live edge} in world $W_j$ if\/
$\mathrm{outcome}_{w,j} = \text{transfer to }u$.
\end{definition}

Under this construction, coupon $j$ seeded at $s_j$ activates node $u$
if and only if the following two events both occur:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item $r_{u,j} \leq p_u^a$, i.e., $u$ adopts in world $W_j$
    (event $A_{u,j}$); and
  \item there is a live-edge path $s_j \to w_1 \to \cdots \to u$ in
    $W_j$.
\end{enumerate}
We denote the conjunction of these two events by
$\mathrm{Adopt}_{u,j}(s_j)$.
Notice that the adoption event $A_{u,j}$ depends only on $r_{u,j}$,
whereas the live-edge reachability event depends only on the random
choices of nodes other than $u$.
Thus the possible-world construction cleanly separates whether a coupon
can reach a node from whether the node adopts the coupon.
For a seed tuple $S=(s_1,\ldots,s_k)$, node $u$ adopts at least one
coupon if
\[
  \mathrm{Adopt}_u(S) = \bigvee_{j=1}^{k} \mathrm{Adopt}_{u,j}(s_j).
\]

\subsection{Adoption Spread and the SIDIM Problem}

We define the adoption spread of a seed tuple $S$ as the expected
number of distinct users who adopt at least one coupon:
\[
  \sigma(S) = \sum_{u \in V}
    \Pr\!\left[\mathrm{Adopt}_u(S)\right]
  = \sum_{u \in V}
    \Pr\!\left[\bigvee_{j=1}^{k} \mathrm{Adopt}_{u,j}(s_j)\right].
\]
Without the coupon promotion mechanism, this adoption spread would be
zero.
Thus, from the campaign organizer's perspective, it wants to increase
this spread as much as possible by choosing good initial coupon
recipients, but it certainly has a limited coupon budget.
Technically, we model this as an adoption spread maximization problem
under a budget constraint.
We use $S=(s_1,\ldots,s_k)$ to denote that coupon $j$ is initially
assigned to seed node $s_j$, and we allow two coupons to have the same
seed node unless otherwise specified.
The campaign has a total budget $k$, restricting the total number of
coupons it can issue.
Note that $\sigma(S)$ is not the sum of $k$ individual coupon spreads,
because the same user may be reached by multiple coupons but is counted
only once.

\begin{definition}[SIDIM]
\label{def:sidim}
Given (a) a directed social network $G=(V,E)$, (b) node behavior
parameters $\{(p_u^a,p_u^d,p_u^t)\}_{u\in V}$, (c) edge forwarding
parameters $\{p(u,v)\}_{(u,v)\in E}$, and (d) a coupon budget $k$, the
task of Sequential Coupon Diffusion Influence Maximization (SIDIM) is
to find an optimal seed tuple $S^*$ for the campaign, such that the
total number of issued coupons is $k$, and when the coupons evolve
according to the SCD model, the adoption spread $\sigma(S^*)$ is
maximized. Formally, it is
\[
  S^* = \arg\max_{S \in V^k}\; \sigma(S).
\]
\end{definition}

Note that it may not be wise to select the individually best seed for
every coupon, since different coupons may end at the same adopting user,
which is only counted once in the adoption spread.
On the other hand, selecting seeds only to be far apart in the graph may
not be a good choice either, since a distant seed with a low adoption
probability may contribute little to the final objective.
Moreover, although the objective is to increase the number of adopting
users, the problem is still tightly related to forwarding paths, because
a coupon can be adopted only after it reaches the user through a
single-path walk.
Therefore, SIDIM is non-trivial both in considering overlap among
multiple coupons and in considering the relationship between reachability
and adoption.
The ordered representation $S=(s_1,\ldots,s_k)$ is kept for clarity in
the RIS algorithm (Section~\ref{sec:algo}), where seed $s_j$ is paired
with world $W_j$.

Fig.~\ref{fig:example} is a numerical example to illustrate the SCD
model with two coupons.
There are two independent possible worlds in this example: coupon~1 is
seeded at $s_1$ in world $W_1$, and coupon~2 is seeded at $s_2$ in
world $W_2$.
In world $W_1$, the live-edge path from $s_1$ reaches node $u$, and
the adoption event $A_{u,1}$ occurs, so coupon~1 activates $u$.
In world $W_2$, coupon~2 follows a different live-edge path and
activates node $v$.
Node $w$ lies on the forwarding chains but does not adopt either
coupon, which means that it helps propagation but does not contribute
to the objective.
Therefore, in this realization, the adoption spread is
$\sigma(S)=2$.

\section{Theoretical Analysis}
\label{sec:theory}

We study the theoretical properties of SIDIM in this section.
We first show that SIDIM is NP-hard, indicating that exact optimization
is computationally difficult.
We then show that the adoption spread function is monotone and
submodular, which allows a greedy algorithm to achieve a
$(1{-}1/e)$ approximation if the objective value can be evaluated.
Finally, we explain why direct greedy is still inefficient and motivate
the reverse-sampling algorithm developed in Section~\ref{sec:algo}.

\subsection{Computational Hardness}

The following theorem states the computational hardness of SIDIM.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:nphard}
The SIDIM problem is NP-hard.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
We reduce from vertex cover in cubic graphs.
Given a cubic graph $H=(V_H,E_H)$ and an integer $K$, we construct a
two-layer SCD instance.
For every vertex $x\in V_H$, we create a candidate seed node, and for
every edge $e\in E_H$, we create an adoption node.
If vertex $x$ is incident to edge $e$ in $H$, we add a directed edge
from the seed node of $x$ to the adoption node of $e$ with transfer
probability $p$, where $p\leq 1/3$.
Each adoption node adopts with probability $1$, and all other
probability mass at a candidate seed node, namely $1-3p$, is assigned
to discard.
Thus each coupon chooses at most one incident edge node and then
terminates.

For a selected seed set $S$ of size $K$, let $c_e(S)\in\{0,1,2\}$ be
the number of selected endpoints of edge $e$.
Selecting the same seed node more than once can only duplicate the same
incident-edge distribution; hence, when comparing against the vertex
cover threshold below, it is sufficient to consider solutions that use
$K$ distinct seed nodes.
The probability that the adoption node of $e$ is activated is
$1-(1-p)^{c_e(S)}$.
Since $H$ is cubic, $\sum_{e\in E_H} c_e(S)=3K$ for every size-$K$
selection.
Thus the expected adoption spread is
\[
  \sum_{e\in E_H}\!\left(1-(1-p)^{c_e(S)}\right)
  = 3Kp - p^2\cdot |\{e:c_e(S)=2\}|.
\]
Equivalently, using
$|\{e:c_e(S)=2\}| = 3K-|E_H|+|\{e:c_e(S)=0\}|$, maximizing the spread is
the same as minimizing the number of uncovered edges.
Therefore, among size-$K$ selections, the spread reaches the threshold
$3Kp-p^2(3K-|E_H|)$ exactly when no edge is uncovered, i.e., when the
selected vertices form a vertex cover of $H$.
Since vertex cover in cubic graphs is NP-hard, SIDIM is NP-hard.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Monotonicity and Submodularity}

Let $\mathcal{U}=V\times[k]$ be the ground set of node-coupon pairs.
A set function $f:2^{\mathcal{U}}\to\mathbb{R}$ is monotone if
$f(S)\leq f(Q)$ whenever $S\subseteq Q\subseteq\mathcal{U}$, and $f$ is
submodular if, for any $S\subseteq Q\subseteq\mathcal{U}$ and any
$x\in\mathcal{U}\setminus Q$,
\[
  f(S\cup\{x\})-f(S) \geq f(Q\cup\{x\})-f(Q).
\]
Submodularity means that the marginal value of a node-coupon pair
decreases as the selected set grows.
Maximizing a monotone and submodular set function under a cardinality
constraint can be achieved by a simple greedy algorithm with an
approximation ratio of $1-1/e$~\cite{nemhauser1978analysis}.
Fortunately, unlike the exact popularity ratio objective in PRM, the
adoption spread objective in SIDIM has this structure.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:monosub}
For any SCD instance, the adoption spread function $\sigma(S)$ is
monotone and submodular.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Fix a realization $\mathcal{W}=(W_1,\ldots,W_k)$ of all coupon worlds.
For every node $u$, let $T_u(\mathcal{W})$ be the set of node-coupon
pairs $(w,j)$ such that coupon $j$ would activate $u$ if it were seeded
at node $w$.
For this fixed realization, node $u$ is adopted by seed tuple $S$ if and
only if $S$ intersects $T_u(\mathcal{W})$, where $S$ is viewed as the
set of selected node-coupon pairs $(s_j,j)$.
Thus the contribution of $u$ is the coverage indicator
\[
  \I[S\cap T_u(\mathcal{W})\neq\emptyset],
\]
which is monotone and submodular in $S$.
Summing these coverage indicators over all $u\in V$ preserves
monotonicity and submodularity.
Taking expectation over $\mathcal{W}$ also preserves both properties,
and therefore $\sigma(S)$ is monotone and submodular.
\end{proof}

By the classical result of Nemhauser et al.~\cite{nemhauser1978analysis},
Theorem~\ref{thm:monosub} immediately yields a
\emph{$(1{-}1/e)$-approximation by greedy} for the cardinality version:
starting from $S=\emptyset$, iteratively add the node-coupon pair with
the largest marginal gain until $|S|=k$.
For the tuple form of SIDIM, where coupon index $j$ must be paired with
one seed $s_j$, this coverage view is still the key structural property
used by our partition-greedy RIS algorithm.

However, the above greedy algorithm cannot be implemented directly,
because evaluating $\sigma(S)$ exactly is computationally expensive.
One may compute the adoption probabilities of the $k$ coupon walks by
solving linear systems over the graph, but this costs $O(kn^3)$ for one
objective evaluation and is already prohibitive for medium-size
networks.
One may also estimate $\sigma(S)$ by Monte Carlo simulation, but a
greedy algorithm would require repeated simulations for many candidate
seeds and many rounds, leading to a cost of
$O(k^2 nR\bar{L})$, where $R$ is the number of simulations and
$\bar{L}$ is the expected coupon-walk length.
Therefore, to make the monotone-submodular structure algorithmically
useful, we need an efficient estimator of the adoption spread.
In the next section, we develop such an estimator using $k$-joint
reverse reachable sets, and then build the \SIDIMRIS{} algorithm on top of
this estimator.

%% ============================================================
\section{Algorithm: \SIDIMRIS{}}
\label{sec:algo}

We now design an efficient algorithm for SIDIM based on reverse
influence sampling.
We first define reverse reachable sets for single-coupon diffusion and
show their coverage property.
We then extend this construction to $k$ coupons by introducing
$k$-joint RR sets, which correctly preserve the coupling between the
$j$-th seed and the $j$-th coupon world.
Finally, we combine this estimator with an IMM-style sampling framework
to obtain the \SIDIMRIS{} algorithm.

\subsection{Reverse Reachable Sets for Coupon Diffusion}

The key insight of classical RIS is that influence maximization can be
turned into a maximum coverage problem over sampled \emph{reverse
reachable (RR) sets}.
In this section, we adapt this construction to the SCD model.
The main difference is that a coupon activates a node only when two
events occur simultaneously: the seed reaches the node through a
live-edge path, and the node adopts the coupon in the corresponding
coupon world.

\paragraph{Single-coupon RR set.}
Fix a coupon index $j$ and its possible world $W_j$.
For a target node $u$, we define the reverse reachable set
$R_j(u,W_j)$ as the set of seed nodes that can make coupon $j$ reach
and activate $u$ in this world.
Formally,
\[
  R_j(u, W_j) =
  \begin{cases}
    \{w \in V : \exists \text{ live-edge path } w \to \cdots \to u
      \text{ in } W_j\} & \text{if } A_{u,j} = 1, \\
    \emptyset & \text{otherwise.}
  \end{cases}
\]
The condition $A_{u,j}=1$ is the adoption gate at the root node $u$.
If this gate does not fire, then no seed can activate $u$ in world
$W_j$, and the reverse reachable set is empty.
If it does fire, then $R_j(u,W_j)$ contains exactly the nodes that can
reach $u$ through live edges in $W_j$.

\begin{lemma}[Coverage lemma]
\label{lem:coverage}
Seed $s$ activates node $u$ via coupon $j$ in world $W_j$ if and only
if $s \in R_j(u, W_j)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$\mathrm{Adopt}_{u,j}(s)$ requires (i) $A_{u,j} = 1$ and (ii) a
live-edge path from $s$ to $u$ in $W_j$.
Condition (i) is the adoption gate in the definition of $R_j(u,W_j)$,
and condition (ii) is precisely $s \in R_j(u, W_j)$.
\end{proof}

Lemma~\ref{lem:coverage} is the single-coupon analogue of the standard
RR-set coverage property: testing whether $s$ belongs to
$R_j(u,W_j)$ is equivalent to testing whether seed $s$ activates target
node $u$ in world $W_j$.

\paragraph{Why a naive union fails for $k$ coupons.}
For $k$ coupons seeded at $(s_1, \ldots, s_k)$ under independent worlds
$(W_1, \ldots, W_k)$, node $v$ is activated iff
$\bigvee_{j=1}^k \{s_j \in R_j(v, W_j)\}$.
A tempting approach is to generate the union
$Q(v)=\bigcup_{j=1}^k R_j(v,W_j)$ and check whether any selected seed
falls in $Q(v)$.
This approach is incorrect because it ignores coupon identities.
If $s_i\in R_j(v,W_j)$ for some $i\neq j$, the union test would allow
seed $s_i$ to borrow the possible world of coupon $j$, although coupon
$i$ and coupon $j$ propagate independently.
Therefore, correctness requires a per-coupon test: seed $s_j$ must be
checked only against $R_j(v,W_j)$.

\paragraph{$k$-joint RR sets.}
Based on the above observation, we define a \emph{$k$-joint RR set}
rooted at $v$ as the tuple
\[
  \mathcal{R}(v)=(R_1(v,W_1),\ldots,R_k(v,W_k)),
\]
where $W_1,\ldots,W_k$ are sampled independently.
The tuple keeps the correspondence between coupon index $j$ and its
own reverse reachable set $R_j(v,W_j)$.
For a seed tuple $S=(s_1,\ldots,s_k)$, the sample is covered if
\[
  \bigvee_{j=1}^k \{s_j\in R_j(v,W_j)\}
\]
holds.
This is the basic estimator used by \SIDIMRIS{}.

\begin{lemma}[Unbiasedness]
\label{lem:unbiased}
Let $v$ be drawn uniformly from $V$, and let
$(R_1(v, W_1), \ldots, R_k(v, W_k))$ be generated from $k$ independent
worlds rooted at $v$.
For any seed tuple $S=(s_1,\ldots,s_k)$, we have
\[
  \sigma(S)
  = n\cdot
  \E\!\left[
    \I\!\left[\bigvee_{j=1}^{k} \{s_j \in R_j(v,W_j)\}\right]
  \right],
\]
or equivalently,
\[
  \E\!\left[
    \I\!\left[\bigvee_{j=1}^{k} \{s_j \in R_j(v,W_j)\}\right]
  \right]
  \;=\; \frac{\sigma(S)}{n}.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The expectation is over the uniformly sampled root $v$ and over the
independent worlds $W_1,\ldots,W_k$.
\begin{align*}
  \E\!\left[
    \I\!\left[\bigvee_{j=1}^k \{s_j \in R_j(v,W_j)\}\right]
  \right]
  &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{u\in V}
     \Pr\!\left[\bigvee_{j=1}^k \{s_j \in R_j(u,W_j)\}\right] \\
  &\stackrel{(*)}{=}
     \frac{1}{n}\sum_{u\in V}
     \Pr\!\left[\bigvee_{j=1}^k \mathrm{Adopt}_{u,j}(s_j)\right]
  = \frac{\sigma(S)}{n},
\end{align*}
where step $(*)$ applies Lemma~\ref{lem:coverage} to each coupon world.
The last equality follows from the definition of adoption spread.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Sampling Procedure}

We now describe how to generate one $k$-joint RR sample.
The sampling procedure has three steps: (a) sample a root node $v$
uniformly from $V$; (b) sample the $k$ adoption gates
$A_{v,1},\ldots,A_{v,k}$ at the root; and (c) for every world $j$ with
$A_{v,j}=1$, generate the reverse reachable set $R_j(v,W_j)$ by a
backward traversal over live in-edges.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:kernel} gives the deterministic transition kernel
for one node once the random number $r_{u,j}$ has been fixed.
This kernel is used lazily during the backward traversal so that the
same node-coupon pair has a consistent outcome within the same possible
world.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Single-Node Transition Kernel}
\label{alg:kernel}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Node $u$; world index $j$; parameters $p_u^a$, $p_u^d$,
  $\{p(u,v)\}_{v:(u,v)\in E}$; random number $r_{u,j}$
\Ensure Outcome $(A_{u,j}, D_{u,j}, v^*)$
\If{$r_{u,j} \leq p_u^a$}
  \State \Return $(1,\; 0,\; \emptyset)$ \Comment{Adopt}
\ElsIf{$r_{u,j} \leq p_u^a + p_u^d$}
  \State \Return $(0,\; 1,\; \emptyset)$ \Comment{Discard}
\Else
  \State Find $v^*$ s.t.\ $r_{u,j}$ falls in $v^*$'s transfer interval
  \State \Return $(0,\; 0,\; v^*)$ \Comment{Transfer to $v^*$}
\EndIf
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:rrset} implements the above sampling procedure.
If none of the root adoption gates fires, the sample is a
zero-contribution sample; it is kept as an empty sample in the estimator
rather than conditioned away.
Otherwise, for each world $j$ with $A_{v,j}=1$, the algorithm performs
a backward BFS.
When the traversal examines an in-neighbor $w$ of the current node, it
generates $r_{w,j}$ only if this random number has not been generated
before, and then checks whether $(w,u)$ is the live edge selected by
$w$ in world $W_j$.
If so, $w$ is added to $R_j$; otherwise, this in-neighbor is ignored.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{$k$-Joint RR Sample Generation}
\label{alg:rrset}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Graph $G=(V,E)$; parameters $\{p_u^a, p_u^d, p(u,v)\}$;
  coupon count $k$
\Ensure $k$-joint RR sample $((R_1,\ldots,R_k),\; v)$
\State Sample root $v$ uniformly from $V$
\State Sample $r_{v,1},\ldots,r_{v,k} \sim \mathrm{Uniform}[0,1]$
\For{$j = 1$ to $k$}
  \State $A_{v,j} \gets \I[r_{v,j} \leq p_v^a]$
\EndFor
\If{$A_{v,j} = 0$ for all $j$}
  \State \Return $((\emptyset,\ldots,\emptyset),\; v)$
    \Comment{Zero-contribution sample}
\EndIf
\State $R_1, \ldots, R_k \gets \emptyset$
\For{$j = 1$ to $k$}
  \If{$A_{v,j} = 1$}
    \State $R_j \gets \{v\}$;\ \ $\text{queue} \gets [v]$
    \While{$\text{queue} \neq \emptyset$}
      \State $u \gets \text{queue.dequeue()}$
      \For{each in-neighbor $w$ of $u$}
        \If{$r_{w,j}$ not yet sampled}
          \State $r_{w,j} \sim \mathrm{Uniform}[0,1]$
        \EndIf
        \If{$(w,u)$ is a live edge in $W_j$ \textbf{and} $w \notin R_j$}
          \State $R_j \gets R_j \cup \{w\}$;\ \
            $\text{queue.enqueue}(w)$
        \EndIf
      \EndFor
    \EndWhile
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return $((R_1, \ldots, R_k),\; v)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{The \SIDIMRIS{} Algorithm}

Let $\mathcal{F}$ be a collection of $T$ independently generated
$k$-joint RR samples,
\[
  \mathcal{F} =
  \{((R_1^{(t)},\ldots,R_k^{(t)}),\, v^{(t)})\}_{t=1}^T .
\]
For a seed tuple $S=(s_1,\ldots,s_k)$ and a sample $t$, define
\[
  Y_t(S)=
  \I\!\left[\bigvee_{j=1}^{k} \{s_j \in R_j^{(t)}\}\right].
\]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:unbiased}, we estimate the adoption spread by
\[
  \hat{\sigma}(S) = \frac{n}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}
    Y_t(S).
\]
Estimator $\hat{\sigma}(S)$ can be viewed as a coverage function on a
bipartite graph with node-coupon pairs on one side and $k$-joint RR
samples on the other side.
The difference from classical RIS is that sample $t$ is covered only
when some seed $s_j$ covers the corresponding RR set $R_j^{(t)}$ with
the same coupon index $j$.

Seed selection is a maximum coverage problem under a partition
constraint: for each coupon index $j$, at most one node-coupon pair
$(u,j)$ can be selected.
We use a greedy rule similar to the one used for partition-matroid
coverage problems.
Starting from an empty selected pair set $P$, each iteration chooses the
feasible pair $(u,j)\in V\times[k]$ with the largest empirical marginal
gain:
\[
  (u^*,j^*) =
  \arg\max_{(u,j)\ \mathrm{feasible}}
    \sum_{t=1}^{T}
    \left(
      \I\!\left[
        \mathrm{Cov}_t(P)\vee\{u\in R_j^{(t)}\}
      \right]
      - \I[\mathrm{Cov}_t(P)]
    \right),
\]
where $\mathrm{Cov}_t(P)$ denotes that sample $t$ is already covered by
some pair in $P$.
The resulting selected pairs are then read as the seed tuple
$S=(s_1,\ldots,s_k)$.
The required sample count $T$ is determined adaptively by an IMM-style
stopping criterion.
Let
\[
  \lambda^* = \frac{(8+2\epsilon)\,n}{\epsilon^2}
  \left[
    \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\ln\tbinom{n}{k} + \ln\tfrac{2}{\delta}}
    + \sqrt{\ln\tfrac{2}{\delta}}
  \right]^2.
\]
Algorithm~\ref{alg:sidim-ris} doubles the sample collection in phases and
uses an empirical lower bound $\mathrm{LB}$ on the optimal spread to
determine the final sample size $T = \lceil\lambda^*/\mathrm{LB}\rceil$.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\SIDIMRIS{}$(G, k, \epsilon, \delta)$}
\label{alg:sidim-ris}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $\mathcal{F} \gets \emptyset$;\ \
  $\mathrm{LB} \gets 1$;\ \ $\epsilon' \gets \epsilon/\sqrt{2}$
\For{$i = 1$ to $\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor - 1$}
  \State $x_i \gets n/2^i$;\ \ $\theta_i \gets \lambda^*/x_i$
  \While{$|\mathcal{F}| < \theta_i$}
    \State Generate one $k$-joint RR sample $\mathcal{R}$
    \State $\mathcal{F} \gets \mathcal{F} \cup \{\mathcal{R}\}$
  \EndWhile
  \State Partition-greedy coverage on $\mathcal{F}$
    $\Rightarrow$ seed tuple $S_i$
  \If{$\hat{\sigma}(S_i) \geq (1+\epsilon')\,x_i$}
    \State $\mathrm{LB} \gets \hat{\sigma}(S_i)/(1+\epsilon')$;\ \
      \textbf{break}
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\While{$|\mathcal{F}| < \lceil\lambda^*/\mathrm{LB}\rceil$}
  \State Generate one $k$-joint RR sample $\mathcal{R}$
  \State $\mathcal{F} \gets \mathcal{F} \cup \{\mathcal{R}\}$
\EndWhile
\State \Return partition-greedy coverage on $\mathcal{F}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Theoretical Guarantees}

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:approx}
With probability at least $1-\delta$, \SIDIMRIS{} returns a seed tuple
$\hat{S}$ satisfying
\[
  \sigma(\hat{S}) \;\geq\; (1/2-\epsilon)\cdot\sigma(S^*),
\]
and its expected running time is
$O\!\left(k(k+\ell)(m+n)\log n/\epsilon^2\right)$, where
$\ell = \log(1/\delta)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
\textit{Correctness.} Lemma~\ref{lem:unbiased} ensures $\hat{\sigma}$
is an unbiased estimator of $\sigma(S)$ for every seed tuple $S$.
The feasible seed tuples form a partition matroid over the ground set
$V\times[k]$, since at most one node-coupon pair can be selected for
each coupon index.
The partition-greedy procedure gives a $1/2$ approximation on the
sample coverage objective for monotone submodular maximization under a
matroid constraint.
Using the IMM sampling framework and the standard Chernoff-bound
analysis for RIS estimators~\cite{tang2015influence}, the number of
samples determined by $\lambda^*$ is sufficient to make the empirical
coverage values uniformly close to their expectations with probability
at least $1-\delta$.
Combining this concentration with the $1/2$ partition-greedy guarantee
gives the stated $(1/2-\epsilon)$ approximation.

\textit{Complexity.}
Generating one sample requires up to $k$ backward traversals, one for
each coupon world.
The expected cost of one backward traversal is linear in the size of
the generated RR set, and the IMM-style stopping rule bounds the total
number of generated RR sets in expectation.
Thus the expected running time is
$O(k(k+\ell)(m+n)\log n/\epsilon^2)$, where the additional factor $k$
compared with classical IMM comes from generating $k$ independent RR
sets for each sampled root.
\end{proof}

Compared with classical IMM, the extra factor $k$ is caused by the
independent-world structure of SIDIM: a $k$-joint RR sample must contain
one reverse reachable set for each coupon world.

\paragraph{Diffusion parameters.}
Node probabilities are generated by the degree-based log-continuous
procedure in Algorithm~\ref{alg:gendist}, which encodes the empirical
tendency of high-degree hubs to forward more and to adopt less.
The normalized log-degree $\hat{d}_u$ controls how $p_u^a$ and $p_u^d$
scale with degree.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Degree-Based Node Probability Generation}
\label{alg:gendist}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Degrees $\{d_u\}$; parameters
  $(\mathit{base}_a, \mathit{slope}_a, \mathit{base}_d,
    \mathit{slope}_d, h)$
\Ensure $\{p_u^a, p_u^d, p_u^t\}$ for all $u$
\For{$u \in V$}
  \State $\hat{d}_u \gets
    \bigl[\min\{1,\max\{10^{-5},
    \log(1{+}d_u)/\max_w\log(1{+}d_w)\}\}\bigr]^h$
\EndFor
\For{$u \in V$}
  \State $p_u^a \gets \mathit{base}_a
    + \mathit{slope}_a \cdot (1-\hat{d}_u)$
    \Comment{Low-degree nodes adopt more}
  \State $p_u^d \gets \mathit{base}_d
    + \mathit{slope}_d \cdot \hat{d}_u$
    \Comment{High-degree nodes discard more}
  \State $p_u^t \gets 1 - p_u^a - p_u^d$
\EndFor
\State $p(u,v) \gets p_u^t / d_u$ uniformly across out-neighbors
\State \Return $\{(p_u^a, p_u^d, p_u^t)\}_{u\in V}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Scenario I (Normal):}
    A balanced campaign in which adoption and forwarding coexist at

    Representative of a typical commercial coupon deployment.
  \item \textbf{Scenario II (High Absorption):}

  \item \textbf{Scenario III (High Forwarding):}

\paragraph{Baselines.}
\SIDIMRIS{} is compared against two groups of methods.

\smallskip\noindent\textit{Coupon-aware and structural heuristics.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{MC-CELF}: Greedy with marginal gains estimated by
    $10{,}000$ Monte Carlo simulations of the SCD process, using CELF
    lazy evaluation~\cite{leskovec2007celf}.
    Serves as the quality oracle.
  \item \textbf{1Hop-Sort}: Ranks nodes by expected one-step adoption
    score $p_u^a + \sum_{v \in N^+(u)} p(u,v)\,p_v^a$ and selects
    top-$k$.
  \item \textbf{Alpha-Sort}: Selects top-$k$ nodes by $p_u^a$.
  \item \textbf{DegreeTopM}: Selects top-$k$ nodes by out-degree.
  \item \textbf{PageRank}: Selects top-$k$ nodes by PageRank
    score~\cite{page1999pagerank}.
  \item \textbf{Random}: $k$ seeds drawn uniformly at random;
    lower-bound reference.
\end{itemize}

\smallskip\noindent\textit{IC-model adaptations (model-mismatch baselines).}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{IMM}~\cite{tang2015influence}: Applies the IMM
    algorithm with edge weights $p(u,v)$ as IC propagation
    probabilities.  Included to test whether a state-of-the-art IM
    solver can compensate for model mismatch.
  \item \textbf{OPIM}~\cite{tang2018online}: An online-processing
    variant of IMM with improved practical efficiency, evaluated under
    the same IC assumption.
\end{itemize}

These two IC baselines are crucial for demonstrating that the
structural mismatch between broadcast and sequential diffusion cannot
be overcome by algorithmic strength alone.

\paragraph{Evaluation metrics.}
Each seed set is evaluated by averaging two quantities over $10{,}000$
independent Monte Carlo simulations of the $k$-coupon SCD process:
(a) \emph{adoption spread}: distinct users who adopt at least one
coupon (primary objective); and (b) \emph{total redemptions}: total
coupon adoptions, counting multiply-reached users (secondary metric).
We vary $k$ from 1 to 200.

\subsection{Model Validation: SCD vs.\ IC Baselines}
\label{sec:exp-ic}

Fig.~\ref{fig:ic_compare} isolates the comparison between \SIDIMRIS{} and
the two IC-model baselines.
\SIDIMRIS{} achieves substantially higher adoption spread across all four
datasets and all values of $k$.
The underperformance of IMM and OPIM is not attributable to algorithmic
weakness: both carry rigorous $(1{-}1/e{-}\epsilon)$-approximation
guarantees for the IC model.
The gap arises from \emph{structural model mismatch}.
IC optimization favors high-degree hubs, since broadcast diffusion
amplifies degree-centrality.
In SCD propagation, however, a node forwards to exactly one neighbor;
high out-degree confers no advantage over the single forward it
actually makes.
\SIDIMRIS{}, built around the actual single-path dynamics, correctly
identifies seeds that maximize adoption reach under sequential diffusion.
This result demonstrates that model fidelity — not just algorithmic
sophistication — is decisive for coupon campaign optimization.

\subsection{Running Time Analysis}
\label{sec:exp-runtime}

Fig.~\ref{fig:runtime} compares running time (ms, log scale) as a
function of $k$ across four datasets.
MC-CELF is omitted because it is prohibitively slow even on the
smallest graph.

Alpha-Sort and 1Hop-Sort complete in ${\sim}10^2$\,ms regardless of
$k$ or network size — both reduce to a single linear scan followed by
a sort.
PageRank requires iterative convergence and lands in the
$10^3$--$10^4$\,ms range on larger graphs.
\SIDIMRIS{} reaches $10^4$--$10^5$\,ms on EmailEnron, reflecting the
sampling budget required for the approximation guarantee; consistent
with the $O(k(k+\ell)(m+n)\log n/\epsilon^2)$ bound of
Theorem~\ref{thm:approx}, running time grows with both $k$ and
network size.

The results highlight a clear quality–efficiency trade-off.
Heuristics sacrifice spread quality, especially at large $k$ and under
High Forwarding, where long-range structure matters.
\SIDIMRIS{} pays a higher cost and in return matches the oracle.
For the intended use case — offline seed selection before a campaign
launches — this cost is well justified.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We introduced the SCD model and formalized SIDIM, capturing the
single-path, non-replicable dynamics of digital coupon campaigns that
fall outside the scope of classical IC/LT influence maximization.
The key structural distinction is twofold: the coupon moves to exactly
one neighbor at each step, and a user is activated only upon adoption
— not upon receipt.
We proved SIDIM is NP-hard and that adoption spread is monotone and
submodular, then bridged the gap between theory and practice by
developing $k$-joint RR sets with a provable unbiasedness lemma.
Our algorithm \SIDIMRIS{} runs in near-linear expected time with a
$(1/2{-}\epsilon)$-approximation guarantee.
Experiments on five real-world networks confirm that \SIDIMRIS{} matches
the Monte Carlo oracle while substantially outperforming both
model-mismatched IC baselines and coupon-aware heuristics — validating
that model fidelity is indispensable for optimizing non-replicable
coupon campaigns.

Several directions remain open.
First, extending the model to \emph{adaptive} seeding — where the
seed set of later stages depends on observed early adoptions — could
reduce budget waste in time-sensitive campaigns.
Second, the current model assumes the forwarding target is chosen
uniformly among neighbors; modeling biased forwarding
(e.g., users preferentially sharing with friends who would benefit
most) is a practically important extension.
Third, the connection between the SCD model and random walk theory
(e.g., hitting probabilities and cover times) may yield tighter bounds
on the expected RR-set size, improving the constant in the complexity
of \SIDIMRIS{}.
